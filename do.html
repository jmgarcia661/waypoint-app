Tastes of Dominican Republic

Mangú
Mashed boiled plantains, traditionally topped with fried onions and served with cheese and salami for breakfast.
La bandera
The Dominican Republic's everyday lunch: rice, beans, and stewed meat, named for resembling the national flag's colors.
Sancocho
A hearty, slow-cooked meat and vegetable stew, often made for family gatherings and celebrations.
Tres leches cake
A sponge cake soaked in three kinds of milk, a beloved Dominican dessert.

Top attractions

Zona Colonial, Santo Domingo
The oldest European settlement in the Americas, founded in 1498 and home to the first cathedral, first paved street, and first university built by Europeans in the New World. Diego Columbus's fortress-like residence, the Alcázar de Colón, overlooks the Ozama River, and the whole zone is UNESCO-listed for its remarkably intact layout. Cafes and boutique hotels now fill many of the colonial-era buildings, giving the district a lived-in feel rather than a museum piece.
Punta Cana
The country's most developed resort region, with over 30 kilometers of white-sand beach and calm, turquoise water protected by an offshore reef. It's the Dominican Republic's main all-inclusive resort hub, drawing visitors who want minimal planning and maximum beach time. Bavaro Beach, the most built-up stretch, still keeps enough public access points for independent travelers to enjoy the same coastline without a resort booking.
Samaná Peninsula
A greener, less developed peninsula in the country's northeast, famous for the thousands of humpback whales that migrate to its waters each winter to breed, one of the most reliable whale-watching seasons in the Caribbean. El Limón waterfall, reached on foot or horseback through the jungle, and the palm-backed Playa Rincón round out the appeal beyond whales. It has a noticeably slower, more local pace than the Punta Cana resort strip.
Jarabacoa & the Central Cordillera
A mountain town in the country's cool, green interior, serving as the base for climbing Pico Duarte, the highest peak in the Caribbean, and for whitewater rafting and canyoning through the region's rivers and waterfalls. It's a genuinely different Dominican Republic than the coastal resorts, with coffee farms and pine forest replacing palm trees and sand. The temperature drop alone makes it a popular weekend escape for Dominicans themselves.
Los Tres Ojos
A system of limestone caves on Santo Domingo's outskirts holding several crystal-clear underground lagoons, connected by walkways and a small hand-pulled raft crossing one of the pools. It's an easy, inexpensive half-day trip from the Colonial Zone, offering a cool escape from the capital's heat. The caves' unusual formations and turquoise water make it a popular but still relatively uncrowded stop.
27 Waterfalls of Damajagua
A series of 27 waterfalls in the country's north, explored by climbing, jumping, and sliding down a sequence of natural rock chutes and pools carved by the Damajagua River. Guides lead groups through as many of the falls as time and comfort allow, with most tours covering seven to twelve of them. It's one of the more adrenaline-driven natural attractions in the country, popular as a day trip from the north coast's Puerto Plata area.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a visa for the Dominican Republic?
Not for EU, UK, US, and many other passport holders for short tourist stays — a tourist card fee is usually built into the airfare or collected on arrival.
Which airport should I fly into?
Punta Cana International (PUJ) if heading straight to the resorts, or Las Américas International (SDQ) for Santo Domingo.
When is the best time for whale watching?
Roughly mid-January through March, when humpback whales migrate to Samaná Bay to breed and calve.
Is it worth leaving the resort in Punta Cana?
Many travelers find day trips to Santo Domingo's Colonial Zone or Los Tres Ojos worthwhile, though it requires a longer transfer — weigh it against simply relaxing if time is limited.
