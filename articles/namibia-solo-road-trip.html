Ten Days of Silence: A Solo Self-Drive Through Namibia

By José Maria Garcia · 10-day self-drive, Windhoek loop

I flew out of Geneva not entirely sure what I was signing up for — a rented 4x4 with a tent bolted to the roof, a stack of paper park permits, and ten days of driving myself across a country that is, by most measures, mostly empty. It turned out to be one of the best decisions I've made.

Namibia doesn't reward you the way a city does. There's no queue for a museum, no menu to agonize over. What it gives you instead is space — the kind that makes you feel appropriately small, in the way I wrote about in my About page. If you're weighing whether to do this yourself, here's everything I actually did, what worked, and what I'd change.

Getting there

I flew out of Geneva, which isn't the obvious departure point most guides assume. There's no direct route, so expect a connection — mine routed through Johannesburg before the short hop up to Windhoek's Hosea Kutako International Airport. Build in enough buffer on the way out; a missed connection in Namibia costs you a full day, not a few hours, since flights in and out of Windhoek aren't frequent.

The car: your home for ten days

I rented through Drive South Africa, who arranged a proper 4x4 with a rooftop tent already fitted — this is, without exaggeration, the single best decision of the trip. You unfold a ladder, climb up, and you're sleeping under canvas a couple of meters off the ground, in one of the few places on Earth where the night sky still looks the way it's supposed to.

A few practical notes if you're planning the same:

  • Book the car months ahead in high season (I went in September, which is dry season and popular) — the good rooftop-tent vehicles get snapped up early.
  • Get the vehicle with two spare tires, not one. Namibia's gravel roads are hard on tires, and you will be far from a garage more often than feels comfortable.
  • Practice putting the tent up and down once before you leave the depot. It takes thirty seconds once you know it; it takes twenty frustrated minutes the first time, usually right as it's getting dark.
  • Budget for fuel more generously than you think — distances between towns are large, and not every dot on the map has a petrol station.

📱 Driving offline

Cell signal disappears for long stretches, and Namibia's gravel roads don't always match what Google Maps expects. What actually worked for me:

  • Maps.me — downloaded the Namibia region before leaving Windhoek; works entirely offline and was reliable for turn-by-turn on gravel.
  • Tracks4Africa — the gold standard for this part of the world; their GPS maps are built specifically for African road conditions and know about tracks that don't exist on standard maps.
  • Google Maps offline areas — downloaded as a backup, mainly useful for towns rather than the open road.

Download everything before you leave Windhoek. Once you're out, you may not see a usable signal again for a full day.

The route

Ten days, one big loop out of Windhoek: north to Etosha for three nights, west through Opuwo into Damaraland for the Brandberg and Spitzkoppe, down the coast to Swakopmund, south into the Namib for Sossusvlei, and back to Windhoek to close the circle. It's a lot of driving — some days five hours behind the wheel — but almost none of it feels wasted, since the landscape keeps changing character every couple of hours.

Etosha: waterholes and waiting

Etosha works differently from other safari parks I'd read about. You don't chase animals — you park at a waterhole, cut the engine, and wait. Over three days that patience paid off again and again: elephants arriving in loose family groups to drink and spray themselves with the pale dust that gives the park its ghostly look, a lone black rhino easing out of the treeline just before dusk, and one unforgettable evening where a pride of lions simply appeared at the water's edge as if they'd been there all along.

The best hour of game viewing I had wasn't a drive at all. It was sitting still at Okaukuejo's floodlit waterhole after dark, watching who showed up.

If you only do one thing in Etosha, make it this: pick a camp with a floodlit waterhole (Okaukuejo is the classic) and simply sit there after dinner. Rhinos in particular tend to move at night, and the stillness makes the whole thing feel less like tourism and more like eavesdropping on something private.

Cape Cross: ten thousand seals, and the smell you can't unsmell

Nothing quite prepares you for Cape Cross. It's one of the largest Cape fur seal colonies in the world, and long before you see them, the sound reaches you — a constant, chaotic barking that somehow gets louder the closer you walk. Then the smell arrives, which is its own kind of unforgettable. Boardwalks let you get remarkably close without disturbing them, and watching pups play in the shallow surf while the adults doze in enormous piles on the rocks is one of those experiences that photos genuinely undersell.

Damaraland: Brandberg and Spitzkoppe

The detour through Damaraland breaks up the long coastal drive with some of Namibia's most striking rock formations. Brandberg, the country's highest mountain, holds ancient San rock art that a local guide can walk you to — worth the short hike. Spitzkoppe, sometimes called the "Matterhorn of Namibia," is smaller in scale but arguably more photogenic, especially the arch formation near sunset, when the granite turns a deep, glowing orange.

Swakopmund and the Sandwich Harbour excursion

Swakopmund itself is a strange, pleasant shock after days of desert — a small coastal town with visibly German colonial architecture, good coffee, and a proper bakery, which after a week of camp cooking felt like an enormous luxury.

The single best organized activity of the whole trip was a day tour I booked through GetYourGuide down to Sandwich Harbour, where the dunes drop directly into the Atlantic. The excursion covered a genuinely wide range in one day: flamingos wading in the shallow lagoons near Walvis Bay, another stop at a smaller seal colony, a thrilling drive up and over towering sand dunes in a specially modified 4x4, and a beach lunch set up right where the desert meets the ocean — a combination of landscapes that doesn't exist together almost anywhere else on Earth.

Sossusvlei and the Namib

The last big stop was the Namib Desert itself, for Sossusvlei and the surrounding dune sea. I stayed at a desert camp just outside the park gates specifically to get an early start — the gates open at sunrise, and the light on the dunes in that first hour is the whole reason to be there. Climbing Dune 45 or Big Daddy is optional; simply walking out into Deadvlei, the cracked white clay pan dotted with centuries-old dead camelthorn trees against the orange dunes, is reason enough on its own.

Where to actually sleep

Rooftop camping is wonderful, but doing it every single night for ten days will wear you down more than you expect — the packing and unpacking of the tent, the early wake-ups from the sun, the accumulated dust. What worked well for me was alternating: two or three nights of proper camping, then one night somewhere genuinely comfortable, purely to sleep deeply, do laundry, and reset.

  • In Swakopmund, a guesthouse with real hot water and a proper bed made a noticeable difference to how I felt the next day.
  • Near Sossusvlei, a private desert lodge just outside the park gates was worth the splurge — being five minutes from the entrance at sunrise, rather than forty-five, changes the whole morning.
  • Everywhere else, the rooftop tent was genuinely great — just don't be a hero about it for the entire trip.

🏕️ Booking campsites well ahead

Namibia's national park campsites, especially inside Etosha, are run by Namibia Wildlife Resorts (NWR) and book out months in advance during the dry season (roughly May to October). If your dates are fixed, book these the moment you know you're going — I booked mine nearly six months out and some camps were already tight. Private campsites and lodges outside the parks (Damaraland, Swakopmund, the Namib) have more availability but still fill up fast in peak season, so don't leave those until the week before either.

Facts and small curiosities that stuck with me

  • Namibia has one of the lowest population densities of any country on Earth — you can drive for hours without passing another car.
  • The Namib is considered the oldest desert in the world, at an estimated 55 million years old.
  • Etosha Pan, the vast salt pan at the heart of the park, is so large it's visible from space, and it's dry for most of the year.
  • Namibia was the first country in the world to write environmental protection into its constitution, which shows in just how well-managed the parks feel.
  • English is the official language, but you'll hear German, Afrikaans, Oshiwambo, and several other local languages depending on the region — a legacy of a genuinely layered colonial and indigenous history.

What I'd tell you before you go

  • Get comfortable with gravel roads before this trip if you haven't driven them before — most of Namibia's scenic routes are unpaved, and speed limits on gravel exist for good reason.
  • Carry more water and fuel than feels necessary. Distances are deceptively large on the map.
  • A basic visa was around €82 at the time I traveled — check current requirements before booking, as these do change.
  • Doing this alone was never lonely. Namibia is full of other self-drive travelers doing the same loop, and camps are social places in the evening.

I flew home by way of a short extension in Mauritius, which made for about as sharp a contrast as travel offers — from the driest desert on the planet to warm turquoise water within 48 hours. That's a story for another article.
